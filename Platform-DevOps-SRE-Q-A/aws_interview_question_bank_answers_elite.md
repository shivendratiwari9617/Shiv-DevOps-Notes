# Senior AWS Cloud Architect, Platform, DevOps, and SRE Interview Questions — Principal Interview Answer Bank

## Architecture and governance

### 1. A SaaS company has 40 teams, regulated and non-regulated data, and inconsistent AWS accounts. How would you design account boundaries, organizational units, and a landing zone while migrating existing workloads without disruption?

I would reset the control plane first, not start by moving apps around. The organization needs OUs that reflect governance reality: security, infrastructure, sandbox, non-prod, prod, and a separate regulated branch so SCPs, detective controls, retention, and production access differ cleanly. Workloads should end up in team- or domain-owned accounts, with regulated workloads isolated from non-regulated ones at the account boundary unless there is a very strong reason not to. Shared accounts should stay narrow: log archive, security tooling, identity integration, transit, maybe centralized observability backends. For migration, I would baseline existing accounts in place first—CloudTrail, Config, GuardDuty, Security Hub, IAM Identity Center, KMS standards, tagging, backup posture, and network controls—then move workloads in waves based on compliance risk and dependency complexity. The failure mode I want to avoid is “new landing zone, old undocumented trust paths.” DNS, CI/CD role assumptions, shared secrets, and cross-account service dependencies all need to be mapped before moving anything customer-facing.

### 2. How would you decide which platform capabilities belong in shared accounts and which should be owned by application teams? How would your decision change for a high-risk tenant?

My dividing line is simple: centralize controls and utilities, decentralize runtime risk. Identity, account vending, organization logging, security tooling, artifact registries, and some DNS or observability foundations belong in shared accounts because inconsistency there becomes systemic. Product runtimes, service-specific data stores, and tenant-facing APIs should usually stay with the application team so release velocity, cost, and incidents remain local. For a high-risk tenant, I move the boundary outward. That often means dedicated account, stricter KMS ownership, tighter production roles, more conservative egress policy, and sometimes a separate deployment lane. If the tenant raises regulatory or contractual exposure, I do not leave them coupled to everyone else just because sharing looked efficient in a diagram.

### 3. A team must launch a globally accessible API with 99.95% availability. How would you choose regional topology, deployment boundaries, and failure isolation? What assumptions must the business confirm?

At 99.95%, the monthly downtime budget is only about 22 minutes, so I would not let the topology decision stay abstract. If the business expects to survive meaningful regional events, I would use at least two independently deployable regional stacks, with traffic steering via Route 53 or CloudFront and no shared runtime dependency that can take both out. Stateless layers should be deployable per region; the hard decision is data: active-passive if consistency and recoverability matter more than global write availability, active-active only if the business truly needs it and is willing to absorb the operational tax. I also want per-region deployment boundaries so a bad release cannot go global by default. Before committing, the business needs to answer four things clearly: whether regional failure is in scope, what RPO is acceptable, whether degraded behavior or read-only mode is allowed during failover, and whether the cost of multi-region is justified by the revenue and trust impact of being down.

### 4. How would you design account provisioning and baseline enforcement so teams can self-serve while exceptions remain time-bound, reviewable, and auditable?

I would make account creation a controlled product, not a service desk workflow. A team should submit a small set of metadata—owner, environment, sensitivity, network pattern, cost center—and automation should place the account in the right OU and bootstrap it with baseline roles, CloudTrail, Config, GuardDuty, Security Hub, budget alarms, logging, backup defaults, and required tags. Mandatory controls belong in inheritance points like SCPs, baseline IAM, and org configuration, not in a checklist someone may or may not follow. Exceptions should be a separate path with an owner, reason, expiry, compensating controls, and review history. If an exception has no expiry and no visible owner, it is not governance; it is quiet drift.

## Identity and networking

### 5. How would you design human, workload, and CI/CD identities across AWS accounts using short-lived credentials? How would you audit and revoke access during an incident?

For people, I want federation from the corporate IdP into IAM Identity Center and role assumption with short sessions. For workloads, I want native AWS identity attachment—instance profiles, task roles, Lambda roles, IRSA or Pod Identity—so there are no static credentials quietly copied between repos and pipelines. CI/CD should use a very small number of trusted principals that assume narrow deploy roles in target accounts, with session tags so every action is attributable to a pipeline, repo, and revision. During an incident, audit starts with CloudTrail and STS: who assumed what, from where, with which chain of trust. Revocation has to be surgical: remove group-to-role access for humans, revoke or quarantine the pipeline principal, tighten trust on the target role, or replace workload roles without collateral damage. Identity design is only mature when incident containment does not require a scavenger hunt.

### 6. A cross-account deployment role receives `AccessDenied` although its identity policy permits the action. How would you distinguish trust policy, SCP, permission boundary, session policy, and resource-policy causes?

The first split is whether the failure happens during `AssumeRole` or after the role has already been assumed. If `AssumeRole` fails, the trust policy and any SCP denying `sts:AssumeRole` are the first places I look. If the assume succeeds and the action still fails, I check the effective chain in order: identity policy on the assumed role, permission boundary if one exists, any session policy attached by the caller, SCPs on the target account path, and then the resource policy or key policy if the service supports one. In real incidents, two things burn the most time: an org-level deny nobody remembered and a resource policy that is narrower than the role. The fastest way through is to stop thinking “the policy allows it” and start thinking “which remaining layer can still say no.”

### 7. Two teams in separate accounts need private access to one service without full network connectivity. How would you compare PrivateLink, VPC peering, and Transit Gateway for routing, isolation, DNS, and cost?

For one-service private access, PrivateLink is usually the best fit because it publishes a service, not a network. The provider controls what is exposed, consumers do not get broad route visibility, and DNS can stay clean with private names. Peering is fine when two VPCs need simple low-friction connectivity, but it grants network reachability rather than service-level reachability and tends to age badly once several teams want selective access. Transit Gateway is the right answer when the organization needs a routing backbone, inspection patterns, or many-to-many connectivity, not when two teams just need a private API. The cost comparison is not just AWS line items; it is also the cost of opening too much network surface for a single dependency. PrivateLink often wins that trade even when the endpoint bill is higher.

### 8. Private workloads in one Availability Zone intermittently fail to reach an external API after a routing change. What evidence would you gather across DNS, route tables, NAT, firewall, and application layers before changing anything?

I would prove the failure boundary before touching config again. On the DNS side, I want resolver success, returned IPs, TTLs, and whether the affected AZ is following the same resolution path as the healthy ones. On the network side, I would compare route tables, default routes, NAT path, firewall or inspection next hops, and whether the routing change introduced asymmetric return or unexpected cross-AZ egress. NAT metrics matter a lot here because “intermittent in one AZ” often means connection pressure or a broken egress path, not a universal outage. Then I would look at VPC Flow Logs, firewall logs, and application evidence: connection timeouts versus resets, retry counts, and whether only new connections fail. I never want to respond to a bad routing change by making a second routing change without knowing whether the real problem is DNS, egress translation, inspection, or retry amplification inside the client.

### 9. How would you design and test a production ingress path using AWS edge and load-balancing services? How would the design change for private traffic or non-HTTP protocols?

For public HTTP, I would normally put CloudFront first, WAF in front of the origin, then a regional ALB for application-aware routing and health checks. That gives me edge termination, caching where it helps, request filtering, and a clean regional failure boundary. The health endpoint must represent real readiness, not just “the process is up,” and testing has to include synthetic probes from outside the VPC so the customer path is real. For private traffic, I drop the public edge and use internal ALB or NLB with private Route 53, or PrivateLink when I want controlled service publication across accounts. For non-HTTP protocols, I bias toward NLB because transport behavior matters more than L7 routing. The test plan should include certificate rotation, unhealthy target behavior, rollout failure, source-IP expectations, and failover—not just 200 OK on a sunny day.

### 10. A security team requires emergency production access during an identity-provider outage. How would you design and test break-glass access without creating an unmanaged standing privilege?

Break-glass should be a recovery control, not a hidden admin model. I would create a very small number of protected local AWS identities or roles, bind them to strong MFA, store custody outside normal engineering access, and scope permissions to genuine emergency tasks rather than broad convenience admin. Invocation should require an explicit incident process, and every use should trigger review. The dangerous anti-pattern is leaving powerful standing credentials around and calling them emergency-only in policy docs. I would test the break-glass path regularly: does MFA still work, can the operator actually perform the intended tasks, does logging capture the session, and can we revoke or rotate the path cleanly afterward? If it is never exercised, it is not a control. If it is too easy to use, it will become the shortcut everybody knows.

## Compute and EKS

### 11. A workload has bursty HTTP traffic, long-running jobs, and a stateful dependency. How would you evaluate EC2, ECS, EKS, and Lambda for each component, including scaling, limits, and operating costs?

I would split the architecture by workload shape instead of forcing one platform. Bursty HTTP often fits ECS well if the team wants containerization without Kubernetes ownership, or EKS if the organization already has strong cluster discipline and real reasons to be there. Lambda is attractive for bursty traffic only if concurrency can be controlled and downstream dependencies can survive it. Long-running jobs are usually a poor Lambda fit once runtime, retries, progress tracking, or parallel worker coordination matter, so ECS, EKS, or EC2 are more honest choices. The stateful dependency should push me toward a managed data service unless there is a hard technical reason not to, because running stateful systems on general container fleets usually just relocates toil. EC2 gives full control and full host burden, ECS is often the best operational middle ground, EKS pays off only when the platform truly benefits from Kubernetes flexibility, and Lambda gets expensive in hidden ways when downstream limits become the actual scaling boundary.

### 12. An EKS deployment reports ready pods, but its ALB targets are unhealthy. How would you trace the path from the load balancer to the application and determine whether rollback is necessary?

I would trust the ALB more than the pod readiness gate until they agree. First I’d inspect target group health reasons, health check path, port, matcher, target registration mode, and ingress-controller events. Then I’d verify the Service selector, endpoints, and whether the app is listening on the exact port and path the ALB is probing. After that I’d check security groups, subnet tagging, target type, and whether the app is failing health because a dependency is down even though the pod is technically Ready. If the issue started with the rollout and the previous revision still has a clean recovery path, rollback should happen quickly. In production, “pods are green” is not a reason to keep customers on a broken release when the edge has already told us there are zero healthy destinations.

### 13. Pods remain Pending during a traffic spike despite autoscaling. How would you separate scheduling constraints, subnet IP exhaustion, AWS capacity or quota limits, and node-provisioning failures?

I start with pod events because they usually tell me which family of failure I’m in. If scheduling fails before node requests even matter, I look at requests, limits, node selectors, affinity, taints, and topology rules. If nodes are requested but do not materialize, I check the autoscaler path, Karpenter or node-group events, EC2 launch failures, instance-family constraints, and service quotas. If nodes arrive but pods still do not schedule or attach networking, subnet IP exhaustion jumps up the list—especially in EKS with VPC CNI—because CPU can look healthy while ENIs or secondary IPs are the real bottleneck. I also want to know whether AWS capacity itself is scarce in the selected AZ or instance class. Pending during a spike is not one problem; it is four different classes of problem that happen to share the same symptom.

### 14. How would you plan EKS control-plane, node, and add-on upgrades across production clusters? What compatibility checks, disruption limits, rollout stages, and recovery options would you require?

I would treat EKS upgrades like a platform release train. Compatibility checks must cover Kubernetes version skew plus every add-on or controller that can quietly break data plane behavior: CNI, CSI, CoreDNS, ingress controllers, admission webhooks, service mesh, security agents, and observability agents. I want disruption explicitly bounded with PodDisruptionBudgets, controlled node surge, max unavailable settings, and maintenance windows aligned with business traffic. For nodes, I strongly prefer rolling out fresh node groups over mutating existing ones in place because replacement gives me a cleaner rollback boundary. I would stage from lower-risk clusters to canary production clusters, then wave through the rest with stop conditions tied to scheduling, service discovery, ingress, and application health. If the recovery plan is “debug the add-on set in prod,” the upgrade plan is not ready.

### 15. A Lambda API's tail latency and throttling increase after a release while average latency remains stable. How would you investigate concurrency, downstream limits, configuration, and retry behavior?

That signature tells me the release hurt a subset of invocations, not the whole population evenly. I would compare release changes in memory, timeout, reserved concurrency, provisioned concurrency, VPC config, environment variables, and SDK retry settings before reading too much into averages. Then I would inspect p95 and p99 duration, init duration, throttles, concurrency usage, and downstream metrics like DB connections, DynamoDB throttles, or external API latency. A common pattern is that the release increases parallelism or makes one code path slower, and that only shows up in the tail until retries start amplifying it. I would also look at whether the function or its clients changed retry behavior, because badly tuned retries can convert a recoverable slowdown into visible throttle storms very quickly.

### 16. An ECS service repeatedly replaces tasks after deployment. How would you distinguish task startup failures, resource exhaustion, load-balancer health checks, and networking from an application regression?

I would break the lifecycle into pre-start, warm-up, and traffic-serving phases. If tasks fail before they ever stabilize, I check image pulls, secret retrieval, IAM, config, and exit codes. If they start and then die, I look for OOM, CPU starvation, or startup-time dependency calls that now fail under the new release. If they stay up but get replaced, load-balancer health checks become the likely culprit: wrong path, wrong port, wrong matcher, or startup behavior that no longer meets the service contract. Networking shows up when tasks cannot reach a required dependency or the load balancer cannot reach them due to security group or subnet mistakes. I call it application regression when the infrastructure contract is stable and the new build alone changes startup behavior or health semantics. ECS is usually not being flaky; it is enforcing the contract we told it to.

## Data, delivery, and security

### 17. A checkout service needs transactional writes, low-latency reads, and an audit trail. How would you evaluate RDS or Aurora, DynamoDB, and an asynchronous messaging layer against its access and failure requirements?

Checkout pushes me toward correctness-first design. If the write path needs relational constraints, multi-entity atomicity, and predictable transactional semantics, Aurora or RDS is the straightforward core record. DynamoDB is viable if the access pattern is truly key-based, the invariants fit conditional writes and transactions cleanly, and the team is disciplined enough to design around partition behavior rather than discovering it during a peak sale. Low-latency reads can come from read replicas, cache layers, or a DynamoDB-shaped read model. For the audit trail, I do not want the checkout path held hostage by secondary processing, so I would use an outbox or event publication model that guarantees the business write first and lets audit and downstream consumers run asynchronously. The real decision is not SQL versus NoSQL. It is whether the checkout invariants are easier to preserve in a relational core or a very deliberate key-value model.

### 18. An RDS application's latency increases while CPU is normal. Which evidence would you use to investigate connection pools, locks, storage performance, query plans, and application timeouts?

I would treat normal CPU as irrelevant until waits are understood. First I’d inspect connection counts, pool saturation, and whether the app changed pool sizing or opened more short-lived connections after a recent deploy. Then I’d look at lock waits, transaction duration, query latency distributions, execution plan changes, and storage-path metrics like read/write latency and throughput. A lot of database incidents are waiting problems rather than compute problems. I also want to correlate application timeout and retry behavior, because aggressive client retries can turn one slow query family into broad latency even while CPU looks calm. If the evidence says the database is spending time blocked or waiting on IO, more compute is just a distraction.

### 19. A DynamoDB workload throttles despite low aggregate utilization. How would you identify uneven access patterns, mitigate customer impact, and evaluate a longer-term data-model change?

I would assume the table is telling me the key design is lying. Aggregate utilization can look healthy while one partition key, one tenant, or one GSI is taking all the heat, so I want request distribution by key pattern, per-index throttles, and timing around the spike. To protect customers, I’d reduce retry amplification, smooth or rate-limit the hottest paths, and add cache where it makes sense, but I would not mistake those for the long-term fix. The durable work is almost always a model change: better partitioning, sharding, time bucketing, or redesigning an access pattern that concentrates demand on too few keys. DynamoDB punishes “works in test” schemas when they hit real skew in production.

### 20. An SQS backlog grows after deployment. How would you distinguish producer volume, consumer throughput, poison messages, visibility timeouts, and downstream failures while protecting message integrity?

I would compare sent, received, deleted, DLQ movement, and oldest-message age immediately. That tells me whether the queue is filling because producers got louder, consumers got slower, or consumers are touching messages but not completing them. Then I’d inspect consumer logs for downstream failures, timeout behavior, and whether a small set of poison payloads is being retried over and over. Visibility timeout deserves special attention because if it is too short, healthy work looks duplicated; if it is too long, stuck consumers hide. The discipline here is not to panic-delete or replay blindly. SQS is often the only thing preserving correctness while the service behind it is failing. I want to keep that integrity intact while I narrow whether volume, throughput, or payload-specific failure is really driving the backlog.

### 21. How would you build a cross-account pipeline that promotes the same artifact through environments, limits credentials, and supports verifiable rollback and auditability?

I would build the artifact once, sign or attest it, and promote that exact binary through environment accounts. The pipeline should assume narrow deploy roles in each target account using short-lived sessions, with the target account owning the deploy permission boundary so the central system cannot silently become broad admin. Promotion should be explicit, not branch magic. Rollback should redeploy the last known good promoted artifact, not rebuild “the same code” from a repo that may already have moved on. Auditability means I can answer exactly which artifact digest, source revision, approval or automated gate, assumed role, and target environment were involved in a given release. If that answer requires piecing together Git history, CI logs, and someone’s memory, the delivery system is not ready for principal-level reliability expectations.

### 22. An infrastructure deployment partially succeeds and leaves production inconsistent. How would you establish the actual state, prevent conflicting changes, and safely reconcile code and resources?

I would lock the scene before I reconcile it. Freeze further deploys, block manual fixes, and assign a single owner for state recovery, because partial infrastructure success turns into a bigger outage when three engineers each “fix” a different truth. Then I’d map intended state, IaC state, and actual AWS state resource by resource and classify them as good, bad, or ambiguous. Only then do I choose the reconciliation action: import, replace, taint, targeted rollback, or manual repair followed by state correction. The objective is not merely to restore service; it is to get code and reality back into alignment so the next apply is safe. Running the same change again without that map is how teams teach themselves to fear their own IaC.

### 23. A security alert reports unusual API activity by a production role. How would you use available audit evidence to establish scope, contain risk, and preserve service where possible?

I would build the timeline from CloudTrail first: which principal assumed the role, from where, with what session context, and what APIs followed. Scope means understanding both capability and actual use: what the role can touch and what it touched in the suspicious period. Containment should be precise—revoke the assuming identity, narrow the trust path, replace the role, or isolate the affected resources—because broad revocation can take out healthy production traffic and turn security into the outage. I also want Config history, deploy records, and application logs preserved before cleanup starts, because the easiest way to ruin an investigation is to “stabilize” by erasing the evidence that told us what happened. If the role is shared across humans and automation, that itself is a design flaw exposed by the alert.

### 24. How would you design encryption, secrets rotation, and recovery access for workloads across accounts and regions? What happens when access to a key or secret fails?

I design this around ownership and failure behavior, not just encryption checkboxes. Data services should use service-native encryption broadly, with customer-managed KMS keys where separation of duties or policy justifies them. Secrets should be accessed at runtime through roles, not copied through CI variables or long-lived config. Across accounts and regions, key ownership, key use, rotation authority, and recovery access all need to be explicit. Rotation only counts if the workload can pick up the new credential without a brittle restart dance. When access to a key or secret fails, the service should already have defined behavior: fail closed on sensitive operations, allow short-lived cached use only where that is truly safe, and alert with enough identity context to diagnose the broken path quickly. The first time a team asks “what happens if this KMS grant or secret lookup fails?” should not be during an outage.

## Reliability, cost, and leadership

### 25. For an AWS-hosted customer API, how would you define user-centered SLIs and SLOs from request and dependency signals, choose actionable multi-window burn-rate alerts, and use the error budget to guide release decisions?

I define SLIs from the customer contract, not from infrastructure convenience. For a customer API that usually means successful request rate and latency on the operations that actually matter to users, sometimes broken out by endpoint class or tenant tier so a low-value path does not hide a business-critical one. Dependency signals are supporting evidence, not the SLI itself. Burn-rate alerts should use at least two windows: one short enough to catch a fast-burn incident and one longer to expose chronic degradation without paging the team for every wobble. The error budget must change behavior or it is just decorative math. If a service is consuming budget too quickly, release pace slows, risky changes get gated harder, or reliability work gets funded ahead of feature work. Otherwise the SLO is not a control; it is a vanity metric.

### 26. A service requires a one-hour RTO and five-minute RPO. How would you compare multi-AZ and multi-region recovery options, and what restore or failover exercise would validate the claim?

Multi-AZ handles infrastructure and AZ faults; it does not answer regional recovery. A one-hour RTO is achievable with a warm standby or a well-practiced regional restore, but a five-minute RPO usually means near-real-time replication rather than snapshot-and-pray. So I would compare a simpler multi-AZ primary plus regional restore against a multi-region warm standby, and I would avoid active-active unless the business truly needs it because it adds operational complexity quickly. The exercise has to prove the claim end to end: recover or fail over into the target region, verify the newest durable business transaction, confirm IAM and secrets work, switch traffic, and show a real customer flow succeeding inside the time target. If the test only proves a database promoted, it did not validate the service objective.

### 27. Route 53 failover occurs, but some users still reach an impaired region. How would you investigate health checks, DNS caching, edge behavior, and application readiness?

I would first verify that Route 53 changed state as intended and that the health checks were measuring a meaningful readiness signal, not just a shallow liveness probe. Then I’d work outward: TTLs, recursive resolver caching, client-side DNS behavior, and any edge layer like CloudFront that might keep origin or regional decisions alive longer than the team expected. I would also validate whether the “healthy” region was fully ready, because partial readiness can make failover look broken even when DNS behaved correctly. The investigation should end with a more realistic failover contract: how quickly traffic really converges, which users may continue hitting stale answers, and whether our application is ready before DNS tells the world it is.

### 28. AWS spend rises 35% overnight in a shared account. How would you attribute the increase, contain avoidable cost, and distinguish a legitimate traffic increase from misconfiguration?

In a shared account I go straight to the biggest deltas by service, region, and owner tag because attribution speed matters more than elegance. Then I compare those deltas with business traffic, deploy timing, autoscaling behavior, routing changes, and new resources. Legitimate growth usually has matching customer or edge signals. Misconfiguration usually has fingerprints: retry storms, runaway scaling, NAT or cross-AZ transfer jumps, oversized fleets, or preview environments that never died. I contain first—cap the resource, roll back the change, or kill the obvious waste—then follow with ownership and root cause. Shared accounts make this harder than it should be, which is exactly why mature product companies stop treating them as a long-term production operating model.

### 29. A legacy application must move to AWS with limited downtime and unknown dependencies. How would you discover dependencies, sequence migration, validate correctness, and define cutover and rollback criteria?

I assume the dependency graph is incomplete until runtime evidence proves otherwise. Code review helps, but DNS queries, network flows, database connections, scheduled jobs, certificate use, file shares, and operator folklore usually reveal the real story. I would sequence the migration so identity, observability, and low-risk dependencies move first, then tackle tightly coupled or stateful elements with a rollback plan still intact. Validation has to include business correctness, throughput, latency, batch side effects, and supportability—not just “the process came up in AWS.” Cutover criteria should define exactly what conditions allow traffic shift, what signals trigger immediate rollback, and when rollback stops being safe because data ownership has moved. The worst migration failures happen when rollback exists only as a slide bullet after the hard state transition has already occurred.

### 30. Tell me about a time you balanced security, reliability, delivery speed, and cost in a platform decision. What evidence changed your approach, and how did you measure the result?

Use a decision you actually made; do not present a sample account migration as your own history. State the competing security, reliability, delivery, and cost constraints, your responsibility, and the evidence that changed your initial recommendation. For example, a shared production account might look cheaper until deploy collisions, broad access, or quota contention are measured; a staged move to workload accounts and scoped roles could then be justified. Explain the option you rejected and why, how you piloted and reversed the change if needed, and report real before-and-after values for release lead time, privileged exceptions, change failure rate, incident ownership, and spend. If you did not measure an outcome, say so rather than inventing a result.

## Evidence-based exercises

### 31. Over the same five-minute window, an ALB reports 1,000 HTTP 503 responses, 2,000 successful requests, and zero healthy targets; the pods remain Ready. What would you check first, what additional evidence would distinguish routing from target health-check failures, and how would you restore service safely?

First align the health and request metrics to the same target group and five-minute window: the zero-healthy sample may be a point-in-time value. Inspect target registration and reason codes alongside ALB 503 access logs and target-status fields. An ALB can **fail open** and route to all registered targets when none is healthy, so the 2,000 successful requests do not by themselves contradict zero healthy targets. Distinguish an empty target group or failed registration from failed health checks on registered targets; compare ingress-controller events, Service endpoints, path/port/matcher, security groups, and direct backend requests. Preserve serving capacity and shift to a known healthy target set or compatible prior revision if customer errors correlate with the release. Verify checkout success and target health separately before closing the incident.

### 32. An STS `AssumeRole` call from a CI role returns `AccessDenied` after an organization-policy change. The target role's trust policy allows the caller, but the effective SCPs and session policies have not been checked. How would you identify the denying control and test a narrowly scoped correction?

Capture the failed STS event and the caller's effective session ARN, source account, requested role, and denial details. For a cross-account assumption, verify the caller's identity policy, permission boundary, any policy on the **existing caller session**, and SCPs on the caller account; also verify the target trust policy and its conditions and any applicable explicit resource-side deny. An SCP on the target role's account principally constrains actions by that role **after** it is assumed, not the external caller's `sts:AssumeRole` request. A session policy *passed to the new AssumeRole call* limits the resulting session; it is not the permission that authorizes the caller to assume the role. Compare before/after effective policy and test the smallest correction with the same CI principal and a canary role, then verify the resulting role can perform its intended action without widening other accounts.

### 33. A service has 720 hours of traffic in a month and an availability SLO of 99.9%. What downtime allowance does that imply, how would you calculate remaining budget after 30 minutes of confirmed downtime, and what measurement assumptions could change the result?

At 99.9%, the allowed downtime is 0.1% of 720 hours, which is 0.72 hours or 43.2 minutes. After 30 minutes of confirmed downtime, 13.2 minutes of budget remain. The arithmetic is easy; the measurement contract is where mature teams earn their pay. You need to know whether this is request-weighted or time-weighted, whether all endpoints count equally, whether latency breaches consume budget, whether maintenance is excluded, and whether the source of truth is customer-visible signal or internal infrastructure. Those choices can change the real meaning of the same 99.9%.

### 34. An Aurora failover exercise completes in 40 minutes, but the most recent verified replicated transaction is 12 minutes old. The targets are RTO 60 minutes and RPO five minutes. Which targets were met, what evidence remains missing, and what would you investigate before repeating the test?

Forty minutes meets a 60-minute *component* recovery target; service RTO is unproven until a customer transaction succeeds, measured from the agreed failure or detection point. A 12-minute-old latest *verified* write does not establish that later writes were lost, but five-minute RPO has not been demonstrated. Compare source commit IDs and timestamps at failure with replica position and recovered records to find the newest recoverable committed transaction. If committed transactions older than five minutes before failure are missing, RPO was missed; otherwise verification may simply have been stale. Investigate replication lag under load, write fencing, application dependencies, and the end-to-end verification gap before repeating the drill.

## Product-company practical exercises

### 35. During a checkout campaign, the ALB access-log sample shows `elb_status_code=503`, `target_status_code=-`, and `target_processing_time=-1`. Customer errors began immediately after a deployment. What would you do in the first 10 minutes, which evidence would you request next, and what would make you roll back?

First 10 minutes: freeze changes, assign an incident lead, confirm customer impact, and line the deployment timestamp up against the error spike. `target_status_code=-` and `target_processing_time=-1` tell me the ALB is not getting a usable response from any target, so I would immediately inspect target-group health, service registration changes, ingress or ECS service diffs, and whether the previous revision is still healthy enough to take traffic. Next I want target-health reason codes, deployment diff, readiness or startup logs, service endpoint state, and any security-group changes. I would roll back fast if the timeline matches the release and the prior target set is healthy, because checkout during a campaign is not where I trade revenue for the chance to debug live.

### 36. A platform team must onboard 50 product teams while account creation takes five days. How would you build a paved road for account provisioning, identity, baseline controls, CI/CD, and observability while supporting auditable exceptions? Which measures would show improved team autonomy, delivery speed, and reliability?

I would turn onboarding into a product flow that creates a usable team environment in hours. A team requests an account, automation places it in the right OU, attaches federated identity, baseline security controls, network hooks, budget alarms, deploy roles, CI/CD scaffolding, and standard observability. I would include service templates, ownership metadata, dashboards, alert hooks, and the approved exposure patterns so teams do not spend their first sprint reverse-engineering how the company ships to production. Exceptions still exist, but they go through an explicit expiring workflow with owners and compensating controls. The measures that matter are time from request to first deploy, number of platform tickets per team, paved-road adoption, change failure rate, time to identify the owner during incidents, and how many exceptions linger past expiry. If those do not improve, the paved road is probably just a dressed-up ticket queue.

### 37. A retailer has a 99.95% monthly availability SLO and a 30-minute recovery target for checkout. A proposed multi-region architecture increases monthly infrastructure cost by 80%. What evidence would you require to choose it over a simpler design, and how would you present the trade-off?

I would require evidence that the simpler design cannot credibly meet the checkout recovery target for the failures the business actually cares about. That means historical outage data, restore and failover drill results, dependency analysis, estimated revenue loss per minute of checkout failure, and proof that the team can run multi-region without introducing a new class of operator-driven outages. If the simpler design can restore within 30 minutes for realistic failure scenarios, an 80% uplift may be unjustified. If not, the extra spend may be cheap insurance. I would present it in business terms: what classes of outage the retailer is buying down, how much operational complexity comes with that insurance, and what assumptions must hold true for the 80% premium to have value. No executive needs a CloudFront lecture to make that decision.

### 38. CloudWatch infrastructure metrics remain healthy, but checkout errors rise after a deployment across three AWS accounts. Which customer-facing signals, logs, traces, and synthetic checks would you correlate; how would you enable cross-account investigation without weakening access boundaries; and what evidence would trigger rollback?

Healthy infrastructure metrics tell me almost nothing if checkout is breaking. I would correlate successful checkout rate, payment-step error rate, latency on critical mutations, cart abandonment, and synthetic checkout failures first, then line them up with deploy markers, application logs, and trace spans across the three accounts. Cross-account investigation should happen through controlled read roles or centralized observability backends, not broad emergency admin grants that erase the boundaries we rely on the rest of the month. I want enough access to reconstruct the path quickly without normalizing cross-account sprawl. Rollback becomes the right answer when customer-visible degradation aligns tightly with the deployment window and the logs or traces point to the new version or config path rather than an unrelated external dependency. For checkout, rollback should be a disciplined reflex, not a last resort after everybody has had a chance to speculate.
