# Senior Azure Cloud Architect, Platform, DevOps, and SRE Interview Answers

## Architecture and governance

### 1. A company with 40 product teams has inconsistent Azure subscriptions and compliance requirements. How would you design management groups, subscription boundaries, and landing zones without disrupting existing workloads?

I would inventory subscription owners, data classification, RBAC, policy assignments, network dependencies, and logging coverage before designing a new hierarchy. Management groups should reflect durable governance boundaries, such as regulated versus general-purpose workloads and production versus nonproduction, rather than a changing org chart. Separate subscriptions where policy, quotas, billing, trust, or incident blast radius genuinely differ; use resource groups for lighter ownership boundaries. Baseline existing subscriptions in place first, testing inherited policy and diagnostic changes with canaries before moving workloads. A self-service landing zone should provide identity, connectivity, observability, billing attribution, and exception handling, not merely an empty subscription. Measure onboarding time, control coverage, time-limited exceptions, and customer incidents during migration.

### 2. How would you separate centralized platform services from team-owned resources? When would a subscription boundary offer more value than a resource-group boundary?

I would centralize shared capabilities such as identity scaffolding, connectivity patterns, private DNS governance, audit-log collection, and approved deployment templates, while teams own their service runtimes and data. A resource group helps with resource organization and scoped RBAC, but it does not isolate subscription quotas, billing boundaries, or subscription-level policy decisions. Separate subscriptions when tenant risk, release autonomy, compliance, quotas, or incident ownership require different controls. Shared services should have explicit operators, capacity limits, failure modes, and cross-subscription access contracts. I would test whether a change in a shared capability can disrupt unrelated products before calling the design sufficiently isolated.

### 3. Design a globally accessible service with private data services and explicit regional recovery requirements. What assumptions about availability, data residency, cost, and operations would you validate first?

First establish which failures are in scope: zone, region, identity provider, data plane, or operator error, and obtain an agreed RTO/RPO for the customer journey. Decide where data may reside, which region owns writes, whether read-only operation is acceptable, and whether regional failover may introduce stale data. A global edge such as Front Door can route to independently deployable regional application stacks, but it cannot make a database or private dependency region-independent. I would compare active-passive against active-active using measurable recovery tests, staffing, write-conflict risks, and total cost, not topology diagrams alone. A game day should verify private connectivity, keys, capacity, traffic shift, and a real customer transaction before promising regional resilience.

### 4. An Azure Policy assignment blocks a production release. How would you identify the evaluation scope, restore delivery safely, and manage an auditable exception?

I would inspect the deployment error and policy evaluation to identify the definition, initiative if any, effective assignment, scope, and resource property that triggered the deny. Then determine whether the template is noncompliant, the policy is mis-scoped, or an approved use case lacks a compliant implementation. Prefer a template correction or targeted policy fix; if neither can meet the deadline, use the narrowest permitted policy exemption with owner, reason, expiry, and compensating controls. I would test the fix against the affected resource and confirm other subscriptions remain protected. The platform should track policy-caused lead time and repeat exemptions as signals that the compliant path needs improvement.

## Identity and networking

### 5. How would you design Entra ID, RBAC, managed identities, and privileged access for developers, automation, and workloads across subscriptions?

Human access should follow Entra groups and scoped Azure RBAC, with production elevation through time-bound approval rather than standing Owner grants. Pipelines should use workload identity federation or managed identities with separate deploy roles per environment and subscription. Workloads should use system-assigned or intentionally scoped user-assigned managed identities so credential reuse does not silently expand blast radius. I would maintain a tested break-glass path distinct from routine privileged access and audit both the elevation and its subsequent actions. Success is fewer standing privileges and secrets without slowing approved production changes or incident response.

### 6. A workload cannot read a Key Vault secret even though its role assignment appears correct. How would you investigate identity selection, authorization model, assignment scope, network access, and propagation?

Start with the exact error and the workload's actual principal ID, including any user-assigned identity selection; a correct grant on the wrong identity is still a denial. Determine whether the vault uses Azure RBAC or access policies, then verify the required secret permission, assignment scope, deny assignments if applicable, and propagation timing. Compare DNS resolution and reachability for the intended private endpoint and check vault firewall settings separately from authorization. Vault diagnostics, workload logs, and a request made with the same identity help separate 403 from DNS, TLS, or timeout failures. Apply the narrowest fix and verify the secret can be read without opening unrelated vault access.

### 7. A team requests Owner on a production subscription during an incident. How would you provide appropriate temporary access and verify that it is removed afterward?

Ask which resource and operation the team needs, then grant the smallest applicable role and scope, preferably through just-in-time privileged access with approval and an expiry. If Owner is genuinely unavoidable, record the incident, approving owner, reason, and duration, and monitor all actions during elevation. Avoid turning a temporary incident workaround into a permanent subscription assignment. After recovery, verify the elevation ended, inspect effective role assignments and activity logs, and reduce the recurring incident procedure to a narrower permission set. The goal is rapid customer recovery without leaving a new standing attack path behind.

### 8. A private endpoint works from one VNet but not another. How would you investigate private DNS, resolution paths, peering, routes, and access controls?

From each VNet, query the exact FQDN and compare the resolver, private DNS zone link or forwarding path, returned IP, and TTL. If both resolve the private IP, compare peering, effective routes, NSGs, firewalls, and the intended return path; reachable DNS servers alone do not prove the record is correct. If VNet B resolves the public address, fix the private DNS resolution chain before changing the endpoint. Use connection tests and service-side diagnostics to separate network reachability from authentication. Verify the customer path from both VNets after a narrow correction.

### 9. How would you compare a single hub-and-spoke network with regional hubs for inspection, hybrid connectivity, blast radius, and cross-region traffic?

A single hub reduces initial operations and can be appropriate for a small footprint, but central inspection, DNS, or hybrid links become shared failure and throughput boundaries. Regional hubs improve traffic locality, fault isolation, residency control, and regional recovery at the cost of more routing and policy management. I would model peak and failover throughput, cross-region transfer charges, inspection latency, and ownership of hub changes before choosing. Test loss of a hub appliance or region to see which products actually fail. The architecture should evolve when shared fate and customer latency cost more than operating another hub.

### 10. An application behind Front Door and Application Gateway fails only under load after a configuration change. How would you trace requests, health checks, timeouts, and backend saturation?

Correlate Front Door access and origin-health logs, Application Gateway access and probe logs, and backend request and dependency traces over the same time window. Determine whether failures occur before the origin, at the gateway, or after the backend receives the request. Compare timeouts, TLS and host-header settings, connection limits, WAF decisions, probe thresholds, and backend p95/p99 latency before adding capacity. If the deployment changed routing or health checks, pause rollout and restore the last known good setting when customer impact is clear. Verify the previously failing customer path, not just individual component health.

### 11. ExpressRoute is established, but application traffic intermittently fails. What evidence would you collect to isolate routing, DNS, network devices, and application behavior?

An established circuit proves neither path symmetry nor application reachability. Compare BGP routes and changes, effective routes, DNS answers, packet loss and latency, firewall/NVA session logs, and packet size or MTU symptoms for successful and failing flows. Correlate these with application timeouts, retries, and dependency calls to identify whether failures target particular prefixes or users. Test from both sides of the link and avoid changing routing until the failing path is identified. Customer-facing probes and application success rates should establish recovery after the network fix.

## AKS, compute, and data

### 12. How would you choose between AKS, Container Apps, App Service, Functions, and VMs for services with different runtime, scaling, and operational requirements?

Start with workload shape, operational staffing, startup and scaling limits, networking, data dependencies, and compliance needs. App Service suits many conventional web applications; Container Apps can run containerized services without operating Kubernetes; Functions fits event-driven work when duration, retries, and downstream concurrency are understood. AKS earns its operational cost when the platform genuinely needs Kubernetes scheduling, controllers, or tenancy controls, while VMs suit host-level requirements or legacy migrations. Compare realistic peak-load and failure-mode cost, not just idle pricing. Favor the least complex platform that satisfies the product's reliability and delivery requirements.

### 13. An AKS cluster cannot schedule new pods during a release. How would you distinguish node capacity, pod-network IP availability, quotas, affinity rules, and autoscaler behavior?

I would first inspect pod conditions and scheduler events to distinguish unschedulable pods from scheduled pods stuck during startup or network allocation. For scheduling, compare requests with node allocatable, taints, selectors, affinity, topology constraints, and namespace quotas. For scale-up, inspect autoscaler events, node pool maximums, VM quota and zonal capacity; then evaluate subnet and pod IP exhaustion under the cluster's actual networking mode. A PodDisruptionBudget may block eviction during an upgrade or replacement but does not directly prevent scheduling a new pod. Preserve serving capacity and verify customer SLIs before loosening constraints or expanding capacity.

### 14. How would you compare available AKS networking modes for IP consumption, connectivity, operational complexity, and migration constraints? How would you account for current support status?

I would model peak nodes, pods per node, upgrade surge, and future growth before choosing a mode. Azure CNI Overlay gives pods addresses from a separate overlay CIDR, conserving VNet IPs; traffic leaving the cluster uses node-address translation, so directly addressing a pod from a VNet is not the default contract. Azure CNI Pod Subnet allocates pod addresses from a separate VNet subnet, with dynamic or static-block allocation options; this makes VNet address planning and subnet capacity part of scaling. Azure CNI Node Subnet also consumes VNet addresses for pods, coupling pod density more tightly to node-subnet capacity. I would test private-service routing, source-IP and firewall expectations, network-policy support, and failure diagnostics with the chosen mode. Before promising a conversion or using an older mode such as kubenet, verify the current AKS support matrix and supported migration path for that cluster's version and region; a parallel-cluster migration may be safer than changing production networking in place.

### 15. How would you plan an AKS production upgrade covering cluster components, node pools, add-ons, workload compatibility, disruption budgets, validation, and recovery?

Inventory version compatibility, deprecated APIs, node images, CNI and CSI drivers, admission webhooks, ingress, security agents, and observability dependencies. Upgrade a representative test cluster, then canary production workloads with spare node capacity and realistic PodDisruptionBudgets. Stage control-plane and node-pool changes according to supported version skew and platform constraints, with stop gates tied to scheduling, DNS, storage, ingress, and customer SLIs. Document recovery by failure type: workload or add-on rollback, node-pool replacement, or a separately prepared cluster when a control-plane downgrade is unavailable. A green upgrade status alone does not demonstrate that customers can use the product.

### 16. A Functions application experiences timeouts and cost growth during bursts. How would you investigate concurrency, execution duration, retries, and downstream capacity?

Compare trigger volume, concurrent executions, p95/p99 duration, timeouts, retry counts, queue age, and cost by function around the burst. Check whether a database connection limit, external quota, or lock contention is causing timeouts while more function instances amplify the bottleneck. Determine the function hosting plan and its actual scaling and timeout limits before changing settings. Use bounded concurrency, backoff, dead-letter handling, and idempotent side effects to protect data while reducing customer delay. Validate the fix under representative load and measure successful business events, not only invocation count.

### 17. An Azure SQL or PostgreSQL workload is slow while compute utilization appears normal. How would you investigate connections, blocking, queries, storage, and application retry behavior?

Normal CPU does not rule out connection-pool exhaustion, locks, I/O waits, or slow queries. Compare active connections, pool wait times, blocked transactions, query plans, storage latency, and any recent schema or deployment change. Correlate the database wait class with application timeouts and retries; aggressive retries can magnify a narrow query regression. Protect customers by limiting retry amplification or rolling back a bad change before blindly increasing compute. Confirm both query latency and end-to-end business transactions recover.

### 18. How would you select storage and database services for a workload with specific consistency, performance, data-residency, and recovery requirements?

Write down data invariants, read/write access patterns, throughput and tail-latency targets, region and residency limits, and RPO/RTO first. Azure SQL or PostgreSQL suits relational transactions; Cosmos DB may fit distributed access patterns where its chosen consistency and partitioning model meet the product contract; Blob Storage fits objects rather than transactional records. Account for keys, private access, backup and restore, and replication semantics in each design. Benchmark representative queries and recovery scenarios before choosing on marketing claims or forecast alone. Make data ownership and the maximum acceptable loss explicit to product stakeholders.

### 19. How would you validate that backups for an application spanning a database, storage account, and AKS state meet its RTO and RPO?

Create an end-to-end restore exercise with known committed business transactions and their timestamps before failure. Recover the database and storage to compatible points, redeploy or restore the AKS workload configuration, and verify keys, identities, DNS, and private networking. Measure RPO from the newest *proven recoverable committed write* at the failure time, and RTO when a real customer transaction succeeds, not when a restore job finishes. If only an older point has been verified, the target remains unproven until newer data is checked; do not infer loss from missing verification alone. Record each dependency that delayed recovery and repeat the exercise under realistic load.

## Delivery, security, and operations

### 20. How would you implement cross-subscription deployment using short-lived identities, artifact promotion, approvals, and environment-specific authorization?

Build and test once, record the artifact digest and provenance, and promote that same artifact through environments. For an external CI system, federate only a trusted repository, branch or environment subject into an Entra application or user-assigned managed identity; untrusted pull requests must not be able to obtain the production token. Give each target subscription a separate, narrowly scoped deployment role, with any managed-identity assignment or privileged data access explicitly controlled, instead of giving one pipeline broad rights everywhere. Bind production approval to the digest, reviewed deployment diff, target, and operator, then gate progression on application and customer-health checks. Query each target's actual image or package identity after rollout; if copying across registries changed the manifest digest, retain verified provenance mapping both digests to the same approved build instead of assuming a tag proves identity. Record commit, builder, approved and deployed digests, issuer and deployment identity, approval, target, and result. Rollback should use the last known good target artifact only after checking whether schema or data changes make it unsafe.

### 21. An IaC deployment partly succeeds and a subsequent plan shows unexpected changes. How would you reconcile live resources and code without losing the incident fix?

Freeze concurrent runs and designate one owner for reconciliation. Compare the last approved plan, deployment logs, IaC state if applicable, live Azure resources, and any emergency change that restored customers. Classify each resource as aligned, changed but healthy, missing, or uncertain; do not remove a working incident fix simply to make the template look clean. Import or reconcile state and code with a reviewed corrective plan appropriate to the IaC tool, then run a safe preview before apply. Verify customer health and drift after the correction before reopening parallel deployments.

### 22. How would you choose Bicep or Terraform for a platform, and how would you separately decide whether a GitOps workflow is appropriate?

Bicep is a strong Azure-native choice when ARM semantics and Azure-focused teams dominate; Terraform can fit a multi-provider estate with existing module, state, and execution practices. Evaluate module governance, provider or API coverage, drift behavior, access boundaries, state operations, and skills rather than choosing by syntax preference. GitOps addresses a different question: whether a continuously reconciling controller is appropriate for a particular runtime, commonly AKS. Define ownership so Terraform, Bicep, and a GitOps controller do not fight over the same resources. Test how emergency changes are reconciled and how controller failure affects releases.

### 23. How would you detect and contain unusual activity by a managed identity while preserving evidence and limiting customer impact?

Build a timeline from sign-in evidence where available, Azure Activity Logs, resource diagnostic logs, application telemetry, and deployment history to distinguish a real compromise from a new rollout. Determine the managed identity's effective assignments, accessed resources, and whether its workload is still serving customers. Contain at the narrowest practical boundary by removing sensitive role assignments, restricting the affected workload, or blocking resource access; already-issued tokens may remain usable until expiry, so do not equate role removal with immediate revocation. Preserve logs and resource state before destructive cleanup. Restore the service using a validated scoped identity and confirm suspicious actions and customer impact stop.

### 24. How would you design secrets, customer-managed keys, and private access for a regulated workload? How would you test recovery if a key or secret becomes unavailable?

Use managed identity to retrieve secrets at runtime, scoped RBAC or vault policies, private connectivity where required, and customer-managed keys where the compliance or separation-of-duties case justifies their availability cost. Document key and secret ownership, rotation, retention, recovery permissions, and behavior when a lookup fails. In a nonproduction recovery test, simulate key disablement, secret rotation, private DNS failure, and vault unavailability; verify detection, fail-closed behavior, and approved restoration. Ensure keys, secrets, and private paths exist in the recovery region if the service claims regional resilience. Report the resulting customer impact, not just whether a key could be re-enabled.

### 25. A customer-facing service has a 99.9% availability target. How would you define its SLI, build actionable alerts, and connect error-budget consumption to release decisions?

Define the customer journey and count eligible successful requests or business transactions, with explicit latency thresholds, excluded traffic, and a measurement window. Report per-region and critical-customer segments as well as an aggregate so a smaller failing cohort cannot hide. Use short and long burn-rate windows to page on meaningful budget consumption, and monitor telemetry freshness so missing data is not mistaken for success. When budget burn is high, tighten rollout gates or prioritize reliability work with the product owner. An SLO only changes engineering decisions if customer-facing measurements drive it.

### 26. During a regional disruption, failover succeeds for compute but the application cannot safely write data. How would you assess replication lag, dependencies, write ownership, and recovery options?

I would stop declaring recovery complete until the write path is safe. Establish the newest durable write, replication position, application dependencies, and whether the original region could still accept writes. If the target region cannot establish single-writer ownership within RPO, choose a declared read-only or queued-write mode only if the product supports it, rather than risking split-brain. Validate identities, keys, private DNS, and downstream systems in the target region. Resume writes only after a controlled promotion and an end-to-end transaction check; communicate the actual degraded customer experience and data-loss risk.

### 27. Azure spend spikes after an autoscaling change. How would you attribute the increase across subscriptions and resource types and decide whether to optimize or retain headroom?

Compare Azure Cost Management data by subscription, service, region, tag, and time with scaling events and customer traffic. Include related costs such as databases, outbound transfer, logging, and idle capacity, not just added compute. If spend bought necessary headroom during genuine demand, reducing it could harm availability; if the change caused scale churn, retry amplification, or persistent unused nodes, contain it with a controlled rollback or limit. Verify customer SLIs and capacity after every optimization. Agree on a cost and reliability target with the service owner before changing production scaling thresholds.

### 28. An on-premises application relies on Active Directory and a SQL Server database and must move to Azure with minimal downtime. How would you evaluate hybrid identity, connectivity, database migration, and cutover while keeping a viable rollback path?

Map authentication protocols, domain dependencies, batch jobs, database writers, and latency-sensitive calls before selecting the Azure hosting model. Test hybrid DNS, VPN or ExpressRoute, and identity behavior with the application; Entra integration alone does not replace every AD/Kerberos dependency. Run a rehearsed data migration with change replication, validation queries, and a defined cutover window. Decide exactly when Azure becomes the single writer and when rollback to the old database ceases to be safe without reverse replication or reconciliation. Measure customer errors and business transaction correctness during and after traffic shift.

### 29. Tell me about a platform guardrail that met security requirements but frustrated product teams. How did you handle exceptions and measure the resulting developer experience?

Use a real project rather than adopting a fabricated first-person success story. Frame your answer as: "A guardrail prevented [specific risk] but caused [measured team friction]. I owned [your change to the approved path], tested it with [teams], and introduced exceptions with [owner, reason, expiry, compensating control]. We saw [before/after result], then adjusted [remaining weakness]." For example, a private-access standard can be evaluated through time to first deployment, policy-denied releases, exception age, and adoption of the approved network template. Explain how your change preserved the security outcome while improving delivery; be clear about which decisions were yours.

### 30. In an incident involving AKS, a private endpoint, and Azure DNS, how would you assign ownership and prioritize evidence and mitigation in the first 15 minutes?

Name an incident lead and assign separate owners to customer impact, AKS or ingress, DNS resolution, and endpoint connectivity so changes are coordinated. Establish the affected transaction and start time, the last rollout or network change, the FQDN and answers from a healthy and failing pod, and whether the private IP is reachable. If a change correlates strongly with the failure, pause it while preserving logs; do not let all three teams change infrastructure simultaneously. Choose the smallest safe mitigation, checking write ownership before shifting traffic. Confirm recovery with the affected customer path, not only DNS or AKS readiness.

## Evidence-based exercises

### 31. A private endpoint resolves to its private address from VNet A but to a public address from VNet B. Both VNets can reach their configured DNS servers. What further DNS and network evidence would you collect before changing the endpoint?

Compare the exact FQDN, DNS server, returned CNAME/A record chain, TTL, and private zone link or forwarding rules from both VNets. Correct DNS-server reachability does not mean the resolver has the private zone or forwards to one that does. Once B resolves the private IP, verify peering, routes, NSGs, firewall rules, and service authorization before declaring recovery. Do not recreate the private endpoint for a resolution problem. Confirm the application connection from VNet B and ensure VNet A remains healthy.

### 32. An ARM deployment fails with `RequestDisallowedByPolicy` after a management-group policy rollout. How would you identify the applicable assignment and reason for denial, and how would you test a safe correction or exception?

Inspect the deployment error and policy evaluation to find the denying definition, assignment, management-group inheritance path, and evaluated storage or network property. Compare the intended baseline with the actual resource payload to decide whether to correct the template or a mis-scoped assignment. Test the minimal correction in a representative scope; if urgent and approved, use a narrowly scoped, time-bound policy exemption rather than disabling the parent policy. Confirm that the release succeeds and an unrelated noncompliant deployment still fails. Record the owner and expiry so an emergency exception cannot become permanent drift.

### 33. After an AKS upgrade, a service's p99 latency rises from 200 ms to two seconds while request volume is unchanged. What comparisons across nodes, workloads, network paths, and dependencies would you make before rolling back or changing capacity?

Compare slow versus healthy pods and old versus new node pools: CPU throttling, memory pressure, scheduling, node image, DNS, CNI, ingress, and service-mesh or sidecar behavior. Trace p99 requests to see whether time is spent before the pod, in the application, or in a database or external call. Correlate add-on versions and release markers with the latency step change; unchanged traffic does not rule out a capacity regression, but more nodes should not be the first assumption. If the cause tracks a reversible node or add-on change, stop the wave and roll back that component while watching customer latency. Confirm the same customer path recovers without hiding the bottleneck elsewhere.

### 34. During a DR test, database recovery takes 50 minutes and the latest verified data is eight minutes old. The service targets an RTO of 60 minutes and an RPO of five minutes. How would you assess the results and verify that the whole application, not only the database, meets the targets?

Fifty minutes shows that the database restore completed within 60 minutes; it does not prove service RTO was met. An eight-minute-old *latest verified* recovery point means the five-minute RPO has not been demonstrated, but it does not alone prove newer committed writes were lost. Compare source commit timestamps or transaction IDs with restored data to establish the actual newest recoverable write and quantify any missing customer transactions. Then verify keys, identity, network, AKS workloads, and a real business transaction, measuring until the full service is usable. Report component and service RTO separately and rerun the test if data verification was stale.

## Product-company practical exercises

### 35. After an Azure Policy rollout, a Bicep deployment returns `RequestDisallowedByPolicy` for a storage account. The application team needs a release within an hour. What would you inspect first, and how would you restore delivery without weakening controls for unrelated subscriptions?

Inspect the exact denied storage property and effective policy assignment before modifying permissions or disabling the guardrail. If Bicep can satisfy the policy safely, make that change and rerun the preview. If the policy rollout was mis-scoped, correct the assignment with a canary; otherwise request a narrowly scoped exemption with owner, expiry, and compensating control. Test that the team's deployment works while noncompliant resources in unrelated subscriptions remain blocked. Communicate the remaining release risk and capture repeated exception requests for platform improvement.

### 36. A checkout API's logs show `HTTP 502` at Application Gateway, while the application's request logs show no matching request IDs. What would you investigate before changing the backend, and which observation would confirm recovery?

Missing matching application logs suggest a failure before request handling, but sampling or a broken correlation path can produce the same observation. Check Application Gateway backend health and probe status, listener and route configuration, TLS/SNI and host headers, backend connectivity, and timeout logs; verify whether the gateway forwarded the requests at all. Compare raw backend access logs with application telemetry before concluding the backend never saw traffic. If a recent gateway change caused the failures, revert it narrowly while monitoring checkout success. Recovery requires the previously failing requests to reach the application and complete customer transactions, not merely a healthy probe.

### 37. A product team needs a self-service Azure environment in one hour, but the current subscription-provisioning process takes a week. How would you design a measurable onboarding service with security controls, ownership, failure recovery, and a time-bound exception process?

Collect only the inputs needed to select the landing-zone archetype: team owner, cost center, data sensitivity, environment, and required connectivity. An idempotent workflow should create or assign the subscription, apply policy and RBAC, attach centralized audit logs and budgets, supply approved deploy identities and networking, and publish status with a recovery owner for partial failures. A team should receive a tested sample deployment, not just a provisioned subscription. Exceptions need named owners, compensating controls, expiry, and a review path. Track request-to-ready time, first successful deploy, manual intervention, policy-denied failures, and whether exceptions actually expire.

### 38. Front Door returns `502` for customers in one geography while aggregate Application Insights dependency metrics look healthy. How would you establish customer scope, correlate regional routing and backend evidence, choose a mitigation, and verify recovery without risking data consistency?

Break out error and latency metrics by geography, edge route, origin group, and backend region rather than trusting aggregate dependency health. Correlate Front Door access and health logs with backend and gateway logs and a synthetic request from the affected geography to find where the 502 originates. If an alternate region can safely serve that traffic and has capacity and correct data, shift the affected route; otherwise consider read-only or bounded degraded behavior rather than risking conflicting writes. Keep one incident lead coordinating edge and backend changes. Verify recovered checkout success and latency for the impacted geography and confirm unaffected regions remain stable.
